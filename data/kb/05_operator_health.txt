CAT 320 EXCAVATOR — OPERATOR HEALTH AND WELLNESS GUIDE

FATIGUE MANAGEMENT
Operator fatigue is responsible for approximately 20% of heavy equipment accidents on construction sites. Fatigue impairs reaction time, decision-making, and spatial awareness at least as much as alcohol. After 17 continuous waking hours, cognitive performance is equivalent to a blood alcohol level of 0.05%. After 24 hours without sleep, performance equals 0.10% blood alcohol.
Maximum recommended continuous operating time before a break is 2 hours for high-intensity tasks such as rock breaking or trenching in tight spaces, and 4 hours for general excavation.
Take a 20-30 minute break every 2-4 hours. During the break, leave the cab, walk around, stretch, and avoid looking at a phone screen if possible.
Night shift operators must sleep for a minimum of 8 hours before their shift starts. Avoid shift rotation that requires switching between day and night shifts more than once per week.
Warning signs of fatigue that require an immediate stop: eyes closing involuntarily (microsleeps), difficulty remembering the last few minutes of operation, missing signals from the spotter, unusual control inputs, feeling that the machine is tilting when it is not.
If you experience any of these signs, park the machine, lower the boom, engage the park brake, and inform your supervisor. Do not return to the cab until you have rested.

HYDRATION
Dehydration of just 1-2% of body weight causes measurable reduction in concentration and coordination. At 5% dehydration, strength and endurance fall by 30%.
Drink at least 500 mL of water per hour when working in temperatures above 25 degrees Celsius. In temperatures above 35 degrees, increase to 750 mL per hour.
Avoid caffeinated drinks as the primary hydration source — while coffee and energy drinks temporarily increase alertness, they increase urine output and worsen dehydration.
Signs of dehydration: dark yellow urine, headache, dizziness, dry mouth, reduced concentration. If you experience these, stop work and rehydrate before continuing.
Keep a 2-litre water bottle in the cab and finish it every 4 hours. Pre-hydrate before a night shift by drinking at least 1 litre in the 2 hours before starting work.

HEAT STRESS
Heat stress is a serious medical risk for operators in hot climates or during summer months, even inside an air-conditioned cab, since leaving and entering the cab exposes operators to extreme temperatures.
Heat cramps: muscle spasms, usually in legs — move to shade, rehydrate with electrolyte drink, rest 30 minutes.
Heat exhaustion: heavy sweating, pale skin, fast weak pulse, nausea, dizziness — move to cool shade, lie down with feet elevated, apply cool wet towels, drink electrolytes if conscious. Requires rest of minimum 2 hours before returning to work.
Heat stroke: confusion, no sweating, hot dry red skin, loss of consciousness — this is a life-threatening emergency. Call ambulance immediately. Cool the person rapidly with water and fanning. Do not leave them alone.
Prevent heat stress: use air conditioning in cab at all times. During outdoor breaks, sit in shade. Avoid meals high in protein during work hours — protein increases metabolic heat. Acclimatise gradually over 7-10 days when starting work in a hot climate.

BACK AND MUSCULOSKELETAL HEALTH
Lower back pain is the most common occupational injury for excavator operators due to whole-body vibration and sustained seated posture.
Adjust the seat before every shift: lumbar support should press firmly against the lower back, armrests should support forearms without raising the shoulders, seat pan should support the full length of the thighs without pressing behind the knee.
Whole-body vibration (WBV) limit under EU Directive 2002/44/EC: daily exposure action value is 0.5 m/s2, limit is 1.15 m/s2. Modern suspended seats on the CAT 320 are designed to keep WBV below 0.4 m/s2 in normal operation.
Stand up and walk for 5 minutes every 2 hours of operation. Stretch your back, hips, and hamstrings during breaks — these muscle groups shorten with prolonged sitting.
Recommended stretches: standing cat-camel (back arch and round, 10 repetitions), hip flexor lunge stretch (30 seconds each side), seated torso rotation (10 each side).
Avoid twisting to reach controls — adjust mirror and seat position so all controls are reachable without twisting.
Report any persistent back pain to the site medic — early treatment prevents chronic injury.

WRIST AND HAND HEALTH
Joystick operation involves sustained gripping and fine motor control. Grip tightness beyond what is needed to control the joystick increases muscle fatigue and risk of repetitive strain injury.
Hold the joystick with a firm but relaxed grip — you should be able to insert a finger between your palm and the joystick handle.
Before each shift, warm up wrists and hands: wrist circles (10 each direction), finger spreads (hold 5 seconds, repeat 5 times), fist clench and release (10 repetitions).
If you experience tingling or numbness in the fingers during operation, this may be early carpal tunnel syndrome — report to the site medic.

EYE HEALTH
Prolonged focus on a single distance such as the digging face causes eye fatigue. Every 20 minutes, look at something at least 20 metres away for 20 seconds (the 20-20-20 rule).
Clean camera lenses and cab windows at the start of every shift — dirty optics increase eye strain and reduce hazard detection.
In bright sunlight, lower the sun visor and wear polarised sunglasses when outside the cab — glare causes temporary vision loss that can persist for several minutes after entering the cab.

MENTAL HEALTH ON SITE
Construction site operators have above-average rates of depression and stress, particularly those working long shifts away from home.
If you feel consistently low in mood, irritable, or anxious, speak to your supervisor or the site welfare officer. Many sites have employee assistance programmes with free confidential counselling.
Isolation in the cab for long shifts is a risk factor — make social connection during breaks a priority. Buddy systems where operators check in with each other during the day have shown measurable mental health benefits.
Warning signs that require taking a break: anger that feels out of proportion to events, inability to concentrate for more than 5 minutes, feeling that the work does not matter or that errors do not matter. These states significantly increase accident risk.

NUTRITION
Large meals reduce alertness during operation — eat lighter meals every 3-4 hours rather than two large meals per shift. High-carbohydrate meals cause blood sugar spikes followed by energy crashes — prefer meals with protein, healthy fat, and slow-release carbohydrates such as oats, vegetables, and lean meat. Avoid high-sugar snacks and energy drinks as a fatigue countermeasure — the energy boost lasts 30-60 minutes followed by a crash worse than the original fatigue. Pack meals and snacks in the cab so you do not skip eating due to time pressure.

BREAK SCHEDULING
Mandatory breaks under most occupational health laws: at least one 30-minute break in any 6-hour shift, and one additional 15-minute break in any shift over 8 hours. Research shows that two 10-minute micro-breaks produce greater alertness recovery than one 20-minute break. During a break, leave the cab and the noise environment entirely if possible. Do not spend breaks on a phone or tablet — screen time does not produce the alertness recovery that physical rest and outdoor light provide.
